I set out to make my strategy more profitable — and disproved my own good idea

Half a day's investigation into prediction markets, slippage, and honesty — and why disproving yourself is often worth more than confirming yourself.

A thin — but possibly real — edge

Some background first. We have a strategy called H6, running on the prediction market Polymarket. Its logic in one line: pick obscure, soon-to-resolve small markets whose outcome isn't yet locked in, and follow the whales in — betting that in these thinly-watched corners, the big players know more than the market does.

After several weeks of out-of-sample validation, it showed a positive, seemingly reproducible edge. But a painfully thin one: after trading costs, roughly +1.5% per dollar. The direction is right, yet that cushion is uncomfortably slim.

Which led to the most natural thought in the world — can we tune it to make more?

The key finding: most of that 3-point gap is slippage, not strategy. Slippage alone eats about two-thirds of the profit. In other words — if you want to earn more, don't rack your brain tuning the strategy; first pin down something we'd actually just been guessing at:

What is our real slippage — 0.5%, 1.5%, or worse?

That 1.5% in the backtest was a made-up number. To move forward, we had to measure it, not keep guessing.

02Concept

What is slippage? Explained through buying vegetables

Don't let the jargon scare you. Slippage = the gap between the price you had in mind and the price you actually pay.

Analogy · The market stall

You want to buy tomatoes at the "going rate." At a busy stall — rows of sellers undercutting each other, plenty of stock — you get them at a fair price, paying barely a cent extra.

But go buy some obscure, about-to-spoil odds-and-ends — where a single seller guards the whole corner — and he names whatever price he likes. Want a little more? He marks it up on the spot. What you actually pay is well above the "going rate."

That extra is slippage. The quieter the market, and the more urgently you buy, the bigger it gets.

Prediction markets are the same. Some markets are busy on both sides with prices held tight — you can trade with almost no loss; others are half-dead, and a single order sweeps the thin resting quotes and immediately jolts the price. The difference is enormous — and we'd never actually measured it.

03The tool

Building a ruler: the slippage probe

Because the system is read-only and places no real orders, "practising execution" here doesn't mean risking real money. It means building an instrument to measure real execution cost: in the kind of markets the strategy would trade, pull the live order book and compute what a $200 order would actually pay, and how much it slips.

Building the ruler hit two traps, both worth recording — because had either gone unnoticed, every measured number would have been wrong:

Trap 1: the book is sorted "backwards"

The exchange returns the sell-order list sorted high to low, so the best price (lowest ask) sits at the end of the array. An existing helper naively took the first element, mistaking the worst price for the best — which is why it kept reporting absurd 196% spreads. Once caught, I switched to robustly taking the true best price and walking the book from the lowest ask up.

Trap 2: dead markets snuck in

The first pass set the volume floor too loose, so a swarm of "weather micro-markets settling that same day" crept in — extremely thin, no real trading — dragging the median slippage to a ridiculous 92%. The live strategy would never touch them. Raise the floor to markets with genuine activity, and the picture snapped back to sanity.

Ruler calibrated, the instrument now runs on a timer every 4 hours, quietly accumulating the real slippage distribution in the background.

04Finding

So what's the relationship between slippage and market category?

The first batch of data came back with a startlingly clean pattern. First, median slippage by market category:

But "category" is not the real cause — it's just a proxy. The actual causal chain runs like this:

Category → price level & book structure → bid-ask spread → slippage

The evidence is hard: in the data, the correlation between spread and slippage is 0.93 — how wide the spread is basically determines how big the slippage is. And what sets the spread? Two things:

One: how far the price is from 0.5. The minimum price increment is one cent. On a market priced at 0.45, a one-cent tick is just 2%; on one priced at 0.02, a one-cent tick is 50%. So the closer to the edge (near 0 or 1), the more the spread blows up in percentage terms. Crypto's median price is only 0.09, precisely because it's full of deep out-of-the-money, lottery-like contracts ("will it reach some far-off price").

Two: whether the money is concentrated or spread thin. Sports handicap lines are engineered by market makers to sit near 0.5 — two-sided, active, tight spreads; whereas the "ladder" markets in crypto and weather smear liquidity across a dozen buckets, each thin and near the edge.

Counterintuitive · Don't be fooled by "busy"

Crypto markets actually have daily volume on par with sports, sometimes higher — yet slippage a dozen times worse. Because their "busyness" is scattered across a long row of prices nobody wants.

So to judge whether a market is tradeable, don't look at how busy it is — look at whether the specific price you want to buy has anyone there to trade with.

05The idea

A seemingly brilliant improvement

Following this pattern, an improvement leapt out on its own — and it looked economically motivated, not mere parameter-fiddling:

Add a price floor to the strategy; trade only balanced markets, and avoid the slippage black hole of edge-priced ones.

I sliced the historical trades by entry price to test the idea. At the time, the data looked like a perfect score:

−27%
Balanced 0.2–0.85 · drop-top
+35.5%

The edge band isn't just a slippage black hole — even its "gross profit" is fake: a 2% win rate, propped up by a handful of jackpot lottery tickets into a falsely high average that turns negative once you strip out the luck. The balanced band, by contrast, has a steady edge spread across many trades — a drop-top of +35.5%. Isn't this a triple win? Shed the priciest cost, shed the fakest return, keep the real thing.

I nearly believed it. But this project has one iron rule that stopped me.

06Discipline

Not so fast — "out-of-sample exams" and pre-registration

That +35.5% was an in-sample finding — I took all the history I already knew and looked back to find a pattern. The problem: you're inventing the rule after seeing the answers, so it's guaranteed to look good on this batch of data — which says nothing about whether it holds on fresh data.

Analogy · The exam

In-sample: summarising a "rule" by looking at draws that have already happened — hindsight; you can spin it any way you like.

Out-of-sample (OOS): taking that rule and using it to predict draws you haven't seen yet, to check if it still works. Only if it still makes money on unseen data does it count.

So before touching the test, I wrote a pre-registration note: nailing down the rule (price floor 0.20), the metric, and the pass/fail line in advance. After the run, you may only rule against it — no changing the criteria afterward. That way the result counts either way, and any reviewer can see exactly what was wagered and whether anyone cheated.

07Reality

An honest slap in the face

I sent the price-floored version (call it H7) into that honest exam machine, tested across 6 rolling windows one by one. The result:

Pre-registered verdict · FAIL The price floor does not improve the gross edge; by its own criteria the direction is disproven out-of-sample.

Where did that seductive +35.5% go? It was a mirage. The "drop-top" metric, at the full-strategy level, already strips out the lottery effect — the single biggest trade it removes is often precisely that jackpot ticket in the edge band. Since the de-luck work is already being done, cutting the whole edge band beforehand is just doing the same thing a second way — adding nothing real.

What looks like a perfect score in-sample shows its true face the moment it steps out-of-sample.
08The twist

The deeper lesson: the ruler can't measure its own value

But this exam also exposed something more important — and not as a way to excuse the failure; a genuine methodological blind spot.

My test gave both the old and new versions the same fixed 0.5% slippage. Yet the entire value of a price floor lies in the fact that "slippage varies with price" (edge band ~7.7% vs balanced ~1.2%). In other words —

I used a ruler that can't measure the value of a price floor.

Under this fixed-cost ruler, the edge-band trades in the original strategy are charged only a "fake low cost," when in reality they'd pay far more slippage. Swap in a price-dependent cost model and the original gets dragged down hard by those expensive trades, while the floored version is barely touched — and only then would the price floor reveal its true net value.

So the accurate conclusion isn't "this direction is dead," but:

Gross-edge improvement
None (disproven)
Net value from saved slippage
Unknown
Next step
Price-aware
cost model

And that "price-dependent cost model" needs to be calibrated with exactly the data the slippage probe is accumulating in the background. So the ordering is right: run the fixed-cost exam first (done — disproved the gross-edge improvement); run the price-aware exam once the probe has two weeks of data. That follow-up test is already pre-registered too.

09Coda

Why disproving yourself is a good thing

Tallying up the half-day: we tried to tune the strategy to earn more, and didn't — OOS honestly told us "the tune-the-strategy road is blocked for now." But in the same process, we dug out that the real bottleneck is "price-dependent slippage," built the instrument to measure it, and incidentally punctured a pretty mirage we'd nearly believed.

This isn't a failure. Disproving "I thought this would make money" and seeing clearly "where the real problem is" is worth far more than blind optimism. The most expensive mistake in quant work is never "failing to find a profitable strategy" — it's "mistaking an in-sample mirage for a real rule and betting real money on it."

Pre-registration and out-of-sample testing aren't there to prove you're smart — they're there to stop you fooling yourself.

Next time around, two things to watch: how much real slippage data the probe has gathered, and — once we finally have a ruler that measures accurately — whether that temporarily-condemned price floor gets acquitted.

Methodology. "Drop-top" is the capital-weighted net return after removing the trade that contributes most to the weighted sum, used to exclude fake edges propped up by a few lottery-like winners.
